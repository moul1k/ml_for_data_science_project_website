THE SUN · BEGIN HERE

Introduction

How do the physical and orbital characteristics of confirmed exoplanets vary, and what relationships exist between planetary properties, host stars, and discovery methods?

An exoplanet is a planet outside the Solar System. Studying these worlds places the planets in our own system within a much wider astronomical context. Confirmed exoplanets are catalogued with information about their sizes, masses, orbits, and host stars. Some planets orbit close to their stars, while others take much longer to complete an orbit. A planet's measured radius describes its size, whereas its mass describes how much matter it contains. Orbital periods describe the time required to travel once around a host star. Stellar measurements add important context to the environments in which planets are found. Different combinations of planetary and stellar characteristics offer a way to compare known planetary systems. The NASA Exoplanet Archive collects measurements from the astronomical literature for scientific use. Its catalogue makes it possible to examine the diversity of confirmed planets while acknowledging that published measurements have different limitations.

Finding a planet around another star is difficult because the star generally dominates the light from the system. Astronomers use several discovery techniques that respond to different observable signals. Transit observations can register changes in brightness when a planet passes in front of its star. Radial-velocity measurements can detect shifts in a star's spectrum associated with its motion. These approaches do not find all types of planets equally easily. Consequently, the known exoplanet population is not a complete census of every planet in the galaxy. A catalogue can also contain missing measurements even when a planet's existence is confirmed. Published estimates may be revised as new observations become available. Comparing the recorded properties of planets and host stars can reveal patterns worth investigating without proving why those patterns occur. Understanding how planets are measured and selected is essential to interpreting any conclusions drawn from the known population.

This project now asks whether the recorded planets form coherent groups in physical-parameter space and which combinations of planetary and stellar measurements explain their variation. Clustering compares proximity without using known planet categories, while PCA and SVD describe the dominant directions of variation and the information lost in lower-dimensional summaries. These methods can identify structure worth studying, but a statistical group is not automatically a distinct formation pathway or composition class. The analysis therefore examines missing-data selection, feature redundancy and sensitivity alongside its numerical results.

Background: NASA Exoplanet Archive · NASA explanation of transit and radial velocity methods. Illustrations created for this project with Matplotlib; no external images reproduced.

Questions driving the investigation

  1. What is the distribution of exoplanet radii?
  2. How are reported planetary mass and radius related?
  3. How are known orbital periods distributed?
  4. How do orbital periods and semi-major axes vary together?
  5. How do observed planet sizes differ across discovery methods?

  7. Do host-star temperatures vary with observed planet radius?

  10. Can selected planetary properties be estimated from other available measurements?

01 / Data Collection

The primary source is the NASA Exoplanet Archive pscomppars composite table, queried through its TAP service at https://exoplanetarchive.ipac.caltech.edu/TAP/sync. The collection script requested 12 fields and returned one catalogue row for each of 6,366 confirmed planets in the archived snapshot.

The Open Exoplanet Catalogue was downloaded as a supplementary raw source and retained for provenance. It was not merged with the NASA table and is not treated as an independent validation sample.

02 / Data Cleaning and Preparation

The pipeline trimmed text fields, removed rows with missing or duplicate planet names, converted measurement fields to numeric values and checked physical validity: positive sizes, masses, periods, distances and stellar values; eccentricity from 0 to 1; and plausible discovery years. No row failed these rules, so all 6,366 records were retained and no measurement was imputed.

Missing values remain explicit and each analysis uses the available rows for its required variables. Orbital eccentricity has the most missing observations (1,065), followed by semi-major axis (430), orbital period (350), stellar radius (328) and stellar temperature (304).

04 / Key Findings and Limitations

01

The catalogue is concentrated toward smaller and shorter-period planets: 61.1% of measured radii are at most 4 Earth radii, and 83.2% of measured orbital periods are at most 100 days.

02

Planet mass and radius are positively related but remain widely scattered (r = 0.451), while period and semi-major axis show the expected much tighter relationship (r = 0.997).

03

Transit discoveries form 74.0% of the snapshot. The observed distributions therefore reflect detection methods and survey history as well as the underlying planet population.

04

Missingness differs across variables, so figures use different subsets. Orbital eccentricity has the largest gap, with 1,065 of 6,366 values missing.

These results describe a selected, evolving catalogue and do not estimate planet occurrence rates across the galaxy. Correlation measures association rather than causation, extreme values can strongly affect statistics calculated on the original scale, and missing measurements limit direct comparison between analyses.

NASA also cautions that composite-table values for one planet can come from different publications or calculations and may not form a completely self-consistent parameter set. Re-running the API request later may therefore produce different values or counts.

VENUS · MODULE 2

Clustering

Do confirmed exoplanets form measurable groups when planetary and host-star properties are considered together?

Fixed NASA snapshot: 20 September 2026. Models use 5,141 complete records and eight numeric features. Clusters are exploratory summaries, not established physical classes.

01Overview02Data Prep03Code04Results05Conclusions

01 / Overview

Clustering groups observations using feature similarity without a response label. Partitional methods assign each row to one of a chosen number of groups. Here, k-means alternates nearest-centroid assignment and centroid updates to minimize within-cluster squared Euclidean distances: WCSS = Σj Σx∈Cj ‖x − μj‖². Multiple starting points reduce dependence on a local minimum.

Agglomerative hierarchical clustering starts with individual planets and repeatedly merges groups into a tree. Average linkage measures the mean dissimilarity across all pairs in the two groups, balancing single linkage's tendency to chain and complete linkage's emphasis on the most distant pair. The full tree is built before choosing a cut.

K-means uses Euclidean distance in standardized, transformed feature space. The hierarchy uses cosine dissimilarity, d(x,y) = 1 − (x·y)/(‖x‖‖y‖), as required by the assignment. Similar directions indicate comparable patterns of deviations from the feature means; cosine ignores vector magnitude and can exceed 1 for opposing directions. Average linkage is compatible with these dissimilarities; Ward linkage would require Euclidean geometry.

The discovery goal is to examine whether size, mass, orbital scale and stellar context produce coherent groups, then inspect those groups in original physical units. No discovery-method category or named planet class is used to determine memberships.

02 / Data Prep

The input is an unlabeled numeric matrix: one planet per row, one measurement per column, with no identifier or categorical field in the fit. From 6,366 cleaned records, 5,141 have complete, finite measurements for all eight features; 1,225 (19.2%) are excluded from this analysis only. The archived raw and cleaned datasets remain intact. No missing value is imputed, and all retained extremes remain in the models.

Logarithms reduce the leverage of orders-of-magnitude differences identified in the EDA; all logged features must be strictly positive. StandardScaler then centers each column and divides by its population standard deviation, giving comparable scales (z = (f − mean(f))/sd(f)). Eccentricity is standardized without a logarithm because circular orbits can have zero eccentricity. Discovery year and distance from Earth describe observation history or location rather than the selected physical question, so they are excluded.

Complete-case selection changes the discovery mix: transit planets rise from 74.0% of the full snapshot to 78.0% of the retained subset, and none of the 289 microlensing planets survives all eight feature requirements. These groups therefore describe the selected, measured population. Inspect the selection audit.

03 / Code

The reproducible Python pipeline fits k-means for k = 2–8 with k-means++ initialization, 30 starts per fit and seed 42. Cosine average linkage is fitted to all 5,141 retained rows. Both methods are evaluated on the same fixed 2,000-row subset for silhouette scoring; memberships and cluster profiles use the full retained dataset. The seed and evaluation indices are saved, and no supervised train/test split or outcome label is used in this exploratory fit.

04 / Results

The silhouette measures cohesion relative to the nearest alternative cluster: s = (b − a)/max(a,b). Values near 1 indicate separation, values near 0 indicate overlap and negative values indicate potentially misplaced rows. Over k = 2–8, k-means has its highest mean sampled Euclidean silhouette at k = 2 (0.441); the corresponding scores for k = 3 and 4 are 0.380 and 0.363. WCSS falls as k increases, so its minimum alone cannot select k.

05 / Conclusions

The selected measurements support a broad distinction between a dominant smaller, lower-mass, short-period group and a minority larger, more massive, wider-orbit group under Euclidean k-means. The hierarchy supports a two-group summary but draws a broader high-radius group, illustrating how the meaning of similarity changes the scientific interpretation.

Five independently drawn 80% subsample refits produce agreement with the reference k-means partition of ARI = 0.981–1.000 when predicting all retained rows. This is a descriptive stability check, not a confidence interval; planets sharing a host are not resampled as a system. Omitting semi-major axis while keeping the same rows and k lowers agreement to ARI = 0.489: correlated orbital features materially influence the partition. Sample stability therefore does not establish stability to feature choice.

Complete-case selection, unequal group sizes, recorded extremes and archive-derived mass/radius estimates all limit a physical taxonomy. The source extract lacks measurement uncertainties and per-value provenance flags, so the analysis cannot separate direct measurements from values inferred through empirical relations. These results generate hypotheses for later study; they do not establish distinct formation mechanisms or habitability.

Implementation references: silhouette analysis and SciPy linkage. Scientific caveat: NASA calculated composite parameters.

PCA

01 / Overview

Principal component analysis rewrites correlated numeric measurements as orthogonal linear combinations. PC1 maximizes variance, PC2 maximizes the remaining variance subject to orthogonality, and subsequent directions follow the same rule. Retaining only the first q components is dimensionality reduction: fewer coordinates approximate each original row.

For a centered matrix B with n rows, the covariance matrix is C = BᵀB/(n − 1). An eigenvector v satisfies Cv = λv and defines a principal direction; its eigenvalue λ is the variance along that direction. The scores are Bv. Larger eigenvalues indicate more represented variance, rather than greater causal importance or better class separation.

High-dimensional data are difficult to visualize, often repeat information through correlated columns, and can produce noisy distance comparisons. This project has only eight inputs, so computational scale is modest; the chief gains are interpretable two-dimensional views and a quantified reduction of redundant information. PCA is linear and can discard scientifically relevant low-variance structure, so the retained share and variable loadings must accompany any projection.

02 / Data Prep

PCA requires a finite numeric matrix with a stated missing-data policy. The same 5,141 complete rows and eight physical features used for clustering enter this analysis: radius, mass, orbital period, semi-major axis, eccentricity, stellar temperature, stellar mass and stellar radius. The seven strictly positive features are log10-transformed; eccentricity remains unlogged. All eight columns are centered and standardized because their original units and scales differ.

Planet names and discovery methods are held in a separate metadata table and do not influence the fitted components. Discovery method colours the score plot only after fitting. No imputation or outlier trimming is applied, and the complete-case selection removes 19.2% of the original rows, including all microlensing entries.

03 / Code

The pipeline fits scikit-learn PCA with the full SVD solver, then independently computes NumPy's economy SVD of the centered matrix. It checks the score and variance identities numerically and fixes the arbitrary sign of each direction consistently for display. A component and its negation describe the same axis.

pca = PCA(svd_solver="full").fit(X_standardized)
scores = pca.transform(X_standardized)
B = X_standardized - X_standardized.mean(axis=0)
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(B, full_matrices=False)
variance = S**2 / (len(B) - 1)

05 / SVD and Reconstruction

The centered standardized matrix factors as B = UΣVᵀ. Here U is 5,141 × 8, Σ is 8 × 8 and Vᵀ is 8 × 8 in the economy factorization. The right singular vectors are the PCA directions, component scores are UΣ, and eigenvalues are λi = σi²/(n − 1). PCA and this centered SVD are two computations of the same structure rather than independent corroborating models.

A rank-q reconstruction Bq = UqΣqVqᵀ keeps the dominant directions. By the Eckart–Young result it minimizes Frobenius reconstruction error among rank-q matrices. The retained squared-singular-value energy equals the cumulative PCA variance here because the analyzed matrix is centered. Direct SVD avoids forming a large Gram matrix and avoids squaring its condition number.

The numerical identity error between PCA scores and sign-aligned UΣ is below 9 × 10−14, and the full-rank relative reconstruction error is below 3 × 10−15. Two components are useful for visualization, while four meet the stated variance target. These errors are computed in standardized log-feature space, not as percentage errors in mass, radius or orbital period.

06 / Conclusions

The observed population has shared variation in planetary size, mass and orbital scale, plus a second direction contrasting stellar properties with orbital period and eccentricity. Four axes summarize most of the selected feature variance, while retaining a distinct eccentricity contribution that would be obscured in a two-dimensional display.

This is a variance-based description of complete catalogue profiles. It does not show that large planets cause hotter stars, establish physical categories or determine planet composition. The retained sample excludes all microlensing planets, the archive can infer missing masses or radii from empirical relations, and both period and semi-major axis encode related information. These choices influence both the component weights and the apparent dimensionality.
